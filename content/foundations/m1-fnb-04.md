title: The Art of Recommendation
---

In Japanese hospitality, a recommendation is an act of care, not a sales tool. When a staff member suggests a dish, a wine pairing, or a menu change, it should feel like advice from someone who genuinely knows the menu and wants the guest to have the best possible experience. It should never feel like an upsell.

The distinction comes down to motivation. A recommendation made in the guest's interest, because you honestly believe they will enjoy it, lands differently from one made to increase the bill. Guests are perceptive. They notice whether a suggestion follows naturally from what they have told you, or whether it seems disconnected from what they have expressed.

## What Makes a Recommendation Trustworthy

To recommend effectively, you need to know the menu personally. Not just the descriptions, the actual flavour profile, the texture, the preparation method, what it pairs with, and what kind of guest typically enjoys it.

If you have not tasted a dish, you cannot recommend it with real honesty. If you have tasted it and it is not to your personal preference, you can still recommend it accurately. Be transparent: "I personally lean toward lighter dishes, but many of our guests who enjoy ○○ find this one exceptional."

A trustworthy recommendation:

- Follows from what the guest has already expressed or ordered
- Is specific, not "the fish is very good" but "the flounder tonight is from the Seto Inland Sea, it's the best I've had this season, and it's only available until the end of the month"
- Is offered once, not pushed, if the guest declines, accept it gracefully and move on
- Is honest, if something is not at its best tonight, say so and point the guest toward the better option


Steering a guest away from a menu item may feel counterintuitive, but it is exactly what builds trust. A guest who learns you redirected them away from an off night will trust every recommendation you make from that point on.

## The Seasonal Recommendation

The most natural recommendation in a Japanese context is the seasonal one. For example: "Tonight's ○○ is at peak season right now, if you enjoy ○○, this is a genuinely good time to try it."

This carries no sales pressure because it is real, time-sensitive information. The guest understands they may not have this opportunity again soon.

## When to Recommend and When Not To

Not every interaction calls for a recommendation. A guest who has clearly decided what they want should have their order taken without interruption.

A guest who is studying the menu with visible uncertainty, or who asks directly "what do you suggest?", that is an invitation. Respond thoughtfully. The standard is simple: offer your genuine opinion when it is invited or clearly useful. Do not fill every interaction with suggestions the guest did not ask for.

## Quiz

**A guest has ordered two light fish courses and is looking at the dessert menu. They ask: "What do you recommend for dessert?" What is the most omotenashi-aligned response?**

- A. Recommend the most expensive dessert on the menu
- B. Recommend the most popular dessert, regardless of what the guest has eaten
- C. Recommend a dessert that complements the light, delicate nature of their meal: ideally the seasonal or in-house preparation: and explain why it suits what they have been eating
- D. Say "everything is delicious" and encourage them to choose

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A trustworthy recommendation follows naturally from what the guest has already chosen. After two light fish courses, something heavy and rich may not be the right ending, and knowing that distinction is exactly what a knowledgeable recommendation looks like. Explaining the reasoning, drawing the connection to the arc of the meal, turns the suggestion into genuine care rather than a sales prompt.
