*Kaizen* (改善) is one of the most widely recognised Japanese professional concepts outside Japan, and also one of the most frequently misread. It is sometimes described as a management philosophy or a quality system, but those descriptions miss what makes kaizen powerful at the personal level. At its core, kaizen is a simple belief: every person, in every role, can make their work a little better today than it was yesterday. When that accumulates over time, the result is a standard of excellence that no single training programme or top-down initiative could produce on its own.

## The Character of Kaizen

改 means "to change" or "to reform." 善 means "good" or "better." Together: to change toward the better. Not toward perfection, toward better. That difference matters more than it might seem. Perfection is a fixed endpoint that no one can actually reach. Better is always within reach. The kaizen mindset fixes its attention on what is possible right now, with what is available right now.

## Kaizen in Hospitality

Small, specific observations turned into small, specific improvements are what kaizen looks like in a hospitality setting:

<div class="keigo-card blue">
• Noticing that guests frequently stop at the same spot in the lobby to ask where the elevator is, and suggesting a small sign that removes the question entirely<br>• Realising that a form's current layout is causing delays at check-out, and proposing a simpler order of fields<br>• Identifying that a handover procedure is consistently losing details, and drafting a clearer template<br>• Discovering through hansei practice that pausing one second before responding to a guest question produces noticeably better answers, and deliberately building that pause in
</div>


Each of these is a small thing. None of them alone is remarkable. A person who does this consistently, and a team that shares and builds on each other's observations, creates a standard that waiting for a training course to fix things simply cannot match.

## Kaizen and Humility

Kaizen asks for honest recognition that however good your current practice is, it can be better. This is not about doubting yourself. The best hospitality professionals in Japan are, without exception, people who believe they still have a great deal to learn, not because they lack confidence, but because they genuinely understand that excellent service has no ceiling.

## Quiz

**A staff member notices that during the breakfast rush, guests who want coffee refills often wait because the coffee pots are kept on a counter that is difficult for staff to reach quickly. Which of the following responses most fully embodies the kaizen mindset in action?**

- A. Mention the issue at the next monthly team meeting so a formal decision can be made
- B. Suggest to the shift leader today that the coffee pots be moved to a more accessible spot during breakfast service, and test whether it improves refill speed starting tomorrow
- C. Accept that the breakfast rush is busy by nature; guests understand there may be delays during peak hours
- D. Ask the manager to purchase an additional set of coffee pots so that backup is always available

**Correct Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Kaizen is characterised by small, immediate, observable improvements based on daily observation. Option B reflects this: the staff member identifies a specific issue, proposes a low-cost change, and tests it in the next shift. Option A delays action to a formal meeting weeks away. Option C accepts the status quo, which is the opposite of kaizen. Option D proposes a solution that requires budget approval and purchasing, bypassing the small, immediate action that kaizen emphasises.
