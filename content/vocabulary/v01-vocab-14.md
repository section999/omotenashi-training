title: 恐れ入ります (osore irimasu)
---

**Pronunciation:** o-so-re-i-ri-ma-su — *osore* is three syllables; the *i* in *irimasu* connects to the *e* ending of *osore* smoothly

**Register:** Highly formal — one of the most refined expressions in professional Japanese. Used to soften requests, express deep appreciation, or acknowledge an imposition with genuine humility.

**恐れ入ります** is difficult to translate directly because it functions differently depending on context. Its literal meaning is "I enter into awe/fear" — *osore* (畏れ / 恐れ) meaning awe, reverence, or humility, and *irimasu* meaning "to enter." The phrase expresses a kind of humble embarrassment at the trouble one is causing or receiving. In English, the closest equivalents are "I am terribly sorry to trouble you" or "I am most grateful" — depending on whether it prefaces a request or a thanks.

The key to understanding *osore irimasu* is that it is always about the self being small relative to the other. When you use it before making a request of a guest ("Excuse me, may I ask your name?"), you are acknowledging that asking anything of the guest is itself a small imposition — and you are lowering yourself before you do so. When you use it to receive gratitude from a guest, you are deflecting the praise with humility rather than accepting it directly.

This phrase marks a truly refined speaker. In premium hospitality settings, *osore irimasu* sets the register above *sumimasen* and even *shitsurei itashimasu*. It says: *I am deeply aware of you as a person, and I hold lightly the privilege of speaking to you.*

## Example Sentences

**1.** 恐れ入りますが、お名前をもう一度お聞かせいただけますでしょうか。
*Osore irimasu ga, o-namae wo mō ichido o-kikase itadakemasu deshō ka.*
"I am terribly sorry to trouble you, but could I ask your name once more?"

**2.** 恐れ入ります。ただいまご確認いたします。
*Osore irimasu. Tadaima go-kakunin itashimasu.*
"Thank you — excuse me for a moment while I confirm."

**3.** 恐れ入りますが、少々お待ちいただけますでしょうか。
*Osore irimasu ga, shōshō o-machi itadakemasu deshō ka.*
"I am terribly sorry to trouble you, but could you wait just a moment?"

## Related Expressions

- **失礼いたします (shitsurei itashimasu)** — "Excuse me" — similar function but slightly less deferential; more common in everyday professional use
- **すみません (sumimasen)** — Casual excuse me/apology; too informal for the register *osore irimasu* implies
- **とんでもございません (tondemo gozaimasen)** — "Not at all / you are too kind" — used to deflect praise or thanks from a guest

## Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is underusing *osore irimasu* — many learners default to *sumimasen* even in formal contexts where the more elevated expression would be more appropriate. In high-end hospitality settings, the register of your language is part of the service. A second mistake is using *osore irimasu* ironically or casually — it should always be delivered with genuine tone and appropriate body language (a small bow, direct eye contact).

## Quiz

**A guest thanks you enthusiastically for your help. How do you respond?**

- A. どういたしまして。(You're welcome — too casual for formal hospitality)
- B. 恐れ入ります。お役に立てて光栄でございます。(I am most grateful. It is an honour to be of service.)
- C. いいえ。(No / not at all — too abrupt)
- D. ありがとうございます。(Thank you — receiving the thanks rather than deflecting it)

**Correct Answer:** 恐れ入ります。お役に立てて光栄でございます。(I am most grateful. It is an honour to be of service.)

**Explanation:** In Japanese hospitality, accepting praise directly can seem arrogant. *Osore irimasu* deflects the thanks with humility, and *o-yaku ni tatete kōei de gozaimasu* expresses genuine honour at being helpful — which is exactly the spirit of omotenashi. The guest feels their thanks were received meaningfully rather than brushed aside.
